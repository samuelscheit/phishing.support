## 2. Eligibility verdict

**Short answer:** Samuel can submit an admissible application as a natural person. The call does not let anyone become a member as an individual expert, though. Membership belongs to an "institution or organisation", and a one-person initiative with no legal entity is not named among the eligible types. It is not expressly excluded either. Being admitted is plausible; being selected is unlikely.

The decisive sentences:

1. **Admissibility** (PDF p. 5, §7.1, criterion 2): "applicant is a natural person aged 18 or over, in a position to apply on behalf of the institution or organisation that is based in an EU Member State or Schengen associated country".
   - Samuel is a natural person. As sole operator he is "in a position to apply on behalf of" phishing.support.
   - Nothing in §7.1 requires the organisation to have legal personality, a registration or statutes.
2. **Who the call addresses** (call page, and PDF p. 3 and p. 5): "This call is open to participants representing public or private universities, research institutions/centres, NGOs and/or other public entities, based in European Union Member-States or Schengen Associated Countries that have experience, capacities and interest in cybercrime-related topics."
   - phishing.support is none of the named types in a strict sense.
   - "NGO" is not a legal form in Germany. A privately run, privately funded, non-commercial service can describe itself as non-governmental and non-profit, but not as a registered NGO.
3. **The member is the organisation, not the person** (PDF p. 4, §4): Members are the "Institution or organisation selected". The representative is "Appointed by their organisation". There is no category for individual experts. This differs from the 2013 calls for the original Advisory Groups, which appointed members "in their personal capacity". The current form's eligibility question still says "applying in a personal capacity as representative of an institution, company or organisation".
4. **The form is wider than the call text:** it offers "Private company" and "Other" as types of organisation and names "institution, company or organisation". So an applicant that does not fit the four named types can still complete the form.

**What this means:**

- **Admissible:** most likely yes, if phishing.support is entered as the organisation and "Other" as its type.
- **Truthfulness of the declarations:**
  - "I have my organisation's support to represent it" is true for a sole operator.
  - "true and accurate" rules out ticking "NGO" in a way that suggests a registered association. Choose **"Other"** and say plainly that it is an initiative run by one person, with no legal entity.
- **Selection:**
  - The panel scores "the type of organisation", EU-funded projects, curricula and prior Europol collaboration. phishing.support has none of the last three.
  - It scores well only on "Developing IT tools and technical deliverables for combating cybercrime" and partly on research.
  - With at most 30 seats and 19 incumbents (mostly universities) able to renew, Europol could fill the call several times over from academia.
- **After selection:** a "cooperation agreement" is signed in Jan–Feb 2027. Europol may want a legal entity as the counterparty. A natural person can sign contracts, but whether Europol accepts that is unknown. **Ask before the deadline** (see the draft inquiry in §3.4).

### 3.3 Expertise and experience

**Research projects related to cybercrime or cybersecurity: Yes**

> phishing.support (https://phishing.support) is an independent, non-commercial service that detects, documents and takes down phishing websites and phishing e-mails, and groups them into campaigns run by the same operator. It has run in production since January 2026; the source code is public under the MIT licence (https://github.com/samuelscheit/phishing.support).
>
> Since January 2026 it has confirmed 283 phishing websites and 38 phishing e-mails and sent about 800 takedown reports to hosting providers, registrars, CDNs and blocklist operators. Volume is uneven: 47 confirmed sites in July 2026, 142 in September, 33 in the first half of November.
>
> Research and development work done so far:
>
> 1. Exfiltration-endpoint discovery. An instrumented browser walks each phishing site the way a victim would and enters only synthetic canary values (RFC 6761 `.invalid` e-mail addresses, unique markers, Luhn-valid card numbers that belong to no one, phone numbers from the fictitious NANPA 555-01xx block). The browser records the network traffic over the Chrome DevTools Protocol without injecting code into the page. An endpoint counts as a collection point only when it actually receives a canary value. This separates attacker infrastructure (Telegram bots, Discord webhooks, collector hosts) from third-party noise.
> 2. Campaign attribution. Sites are linked only by indicators that belong to one operator: the same exfiltration endpoint, the same IBAN or crypto address (checksums verified), or the same kit file. Kit files count only if they are at least 1 KiB, are not served by top-ranked sites and are not published library files (checked by SHA-256 against the jsDelivr CDN). Server IP addresses are recorded but deliberately not used to link sites, because shared hosting produces false links.
> 3. Kit hunting. Hashes of same-host scripts, stylesheets and images from each confirmed site are searched on urlscan.io to find sibling deployments of the same kit before they are reported.
> 4. Evidence standard. Every capture is stored as MHTML plus a screenshot with the URL and time drawn in, a manifest with the SHA-256 of every file, and an RFC 3161 timestamp token from an independent time-stamping authority over the manifest. Each canary visit adds a machine-readable record of every request, a dated protocol, its own manifest and its own RFC 3161 token.
> 5. Referral formats. For each campaign the system drafts a criminal complaint for German prosecutors with every site, the linking indicators, per-provider notes on which subscriber, payment and access data exist and how they can be preserved, CSV annexes, and the full evidence archive.
>
> Written research is kept in the repository, e.g. on criminal-referral routes, cross-border investigation instruments and multi-country coordination of phishing investigations (docs/research).
>

### 3.4 Motivation

**Why would you like to cooperate with Europol?**

> phishing.support finds phishing campaigns early and gets their sites taken down within hours, but takedown does not stop the operators: the same kit is back online under a new domain within hours. Its data shows the same operators again and again, linked by their exfiltration endpoints and kit files, but this work only matters if law enforcement can use it.
>
> I would like to learn directly from EC3 and the AGRD members:
>
> - which data, formats and evidence standards investigators can actually use from a private source;
> - how takedown should be coordinated with ongoing investigations so that reporting does not tip off operators;
> - how campaign data from a small non-profit can be made reusable by researchers and LEAs across Europe.
>
> In return I can contribute a working open-source system, real data on phishing-as-a-service deployments, and engineering time.

**Which projects/researches would you like to develop with Europol?**

> 1. Evidence package for private-sector phishing submissions. A common, open format for what a private party hands to a law-enforcement agency about a phishing campaign: original captures, SHA-256 manifest, RFC 3161 timestamps, a canary-visit protocol, a flat indicator table, and MISP/STIX 2.1 exports of the same data. Deliverables: a specification, a reference implementation (open source) and guidance on chain of custody and on qualified vs. non-qualified timestamps across Member States.
> 2. Exfiltration-endpoint detection with synthetic canary credentials. Turn phishing.support's method into a documented, reusable methodology: how to generate canary data that cannot harm third parties, how to record where it goes without interacting with the page beyond a victim's actions, and how to separate attacker endpoints from noise. Includes a legal and ethical guidance note for researchers and LEAs.
> 3. Linking campaigns and fingerprinting PhaaS kits. Evaluate which indicators reliably attribute sites to one operator (exfiltration endpoint, payment receiver, kit file hashes with library exclusion), and which do not (shared hosting IPs). Measure false-link rates on real data. Deliverables: a research report and an open dataset of anonymised campaign structures, as far as confidentiality allows.
> 4. Takedown–investigation deconfliction. A position paper with recommendations on how private reporters, providers and LEAs can avoid reporting the upstream infrastructure of a campaign under investigation (panel, exfiltration endpoint, payment receiver) while still protecting victims through takedown.
>
> I can provide the running platform, its data under agreed conditions, and development time for each of these.
